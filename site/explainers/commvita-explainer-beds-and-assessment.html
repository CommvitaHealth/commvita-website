1 A bed is only useful if you know when it frees up

Community bed management is mostly a question about the future: how many beds will I have tomorrow. commvita answers it with occupancy now, predicted discharges at twenty-four, forty-eight and seventy-two hours, and the delayed transfers with the reason each one is stuck.

82.1% occupancy across the units, nine beds free, one closed, three delayed transfers. The delays are broken out by reason — one social care, one NHS delay, one awaiting placement — because those three need three different people to fix them, and a single “DTOC: 3” tells nobody which call to make.

Each unit shows its own occupancy: an intermediate care unit, a rehab unit, a palliative unit, a community unit. They have different lengths of stay and different pressures, and averaging them into one number hides the one that’s full.

2 Mental health beds, where the clock is legal

A mental health bed board has a column no other bed board has: the section, and when it expires. A section that lapses without being renewed or discharged is unlawful detention, so the expiry sits on the board next to the bed rather than in a diary, and a tile at the top counts the ones due soon. On the demonstration records it counts none, and a nought there is a real nought, because the tile is computed from the same rows the table shows.

The board groups by ward, marks the patient’s section, and keeps a separate row for an out-of-area placement: a bed this organisation doesn’t hold, occupied by a person it is responsible for. One of the three occupied beds is out of area, which is the number a system is judged on and the one people usually have to ask for.

The board used to say three and list nobody. Reported from the live site: a tile reading three occupied beds under a live chip, above a table with no rows and no message. The endpoint was fine and the page never called it. The build merged on 20 September wired the page to its store, so the tile and the table are now the same rows, and the admission form writes to the register it was already able to write to. The top bar still says demonstration data, which is true of the three records on it.

3 Assessing at home, without a signal

OASIS-E2 is the assessment US home health agencies have to complete at start of care, at follow-up and at discharge. It’s long — thirty-seven items at start of care — and it gets done in somebody’s house, where the mobile signal usually isn’t.

So the queue is offline-first. One assessment is marked pending sync with a banner saying it’ll go up automatically when the connection comes back, and a clinician can sync by hand if they’d rather. Progress shows as items done over items required, so a part-finished assessment reads as 18 of 37 instead of just “in progress”.

One is marked rejected. A rejected submission that isn’t on the screen is a payment that quietly never arrives, which is why it sits in the same queue as the rest.
