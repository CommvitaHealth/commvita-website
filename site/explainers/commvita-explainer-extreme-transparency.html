Extreme transparency, engineered in

The founders wanted a company that never sells a screenshot of a spreadsheet as a module. So the platform states what is live, built or stub, where every number comes from, how far each standard goes and where it stands against others, and a build fails when the words drift from the facts.

Live vs demonstrated: Live — real, API-backed platform logic (wired end-to-end today) Demonstrated — representative control surface with seeded data / illustrative UI mock-up

1 Why we chose it

The founders of commvita have all sat on the buying side of health software. We’ve been shown demos that turned out to be mock-ups, bought roadmaps that never shipped, and been told a module was live when it was a screen over a spreadsheet. We decided this company wouldn’t do that, and then decided that a promise wasn’t enough.

So transparency here is engineered, and this page is about the engineering. The platform states, on its own screens and in machine-checked registers, what is live, what is built but not yet connected, what is a stub, where every number on every page comes from, how it measures against other systems, and what each standard it names means in practice. A build that contradicts any of those statements fails.

The test we apply to ourselves. If a claim can’t be measured from the platform, it doesn’t go on a screen or a page. If it can be measured, the measurement is generated at build time and a check fails the build when the words drift from it. The rest of this page is a walk through those checks.

2 Every page says where its numbers come from

Open any screen and the top bar tells you whether it is reading platform data or showing seeded demonstration data. That badge isn’t typed by a developer. It comes from a register measured from the page’s own source code.

The register measures each page from the page itself, not from what somebody declared about it. A page that both reads the platform and carries demonstration figures is classed as mixed and left there, because which of the two a given tile shows can’t be told apart with certainty, and guessing would be the confidently wrong answer. The build fails only in the direction that could harm someone: a page declared as platform data while reading nothing. A page that understates itself is allowed.

At build B-973 that gives 427 routed pages: 97 reading the platform, 232 mixed and 98 seeded. The seeded ones are split further. Eleven are backend-free by design, a document about the platform or a calculator with nothing to fetch, and 87 owe a backend. Of those 87, the module registry says 41 have no API at all and are roadmap items shown as a design, 16 name an API the page never calls, and for 30 the registry says neither, which the page reports as not recorded instead of guessing. We publish the 98 because a buyer who finds one for themselves stops believing the other 329.

3 Every module says what it is

The module registry carries a status for each of the 634 modules the platform ships: live, built or stub. The words have definitions, and the definitions are printed next to the count.

Live means fully operational with no external dependency. Built means the code is complete and it needs NHS credentials or national onboarding to switch on. Stub means the adapter or scaffold exists and the credentials would activate it. Backlog is a competitive gap that is an area for improvement and not yet built, and it is counted separately so it can never inflate the module total. The registry, the competitive audit and the routing table are checked against one another on every build, so a module can’t appear in one and not the others.

4 Every standard says how far

The Architecture screen lists 145 standards the platform is built to, across NHS, clinical, pharmacy, information governance, interoperability, legal and engineering. Naming a standard is cheap. The screen says what each mapping means.

The alignment against the WHO reference architecture and the European health data space is labelled on the screen as a design-alignment statement and not a certification. Each building block gets one of three words: aligned, partial, or designed to align. Nothing is marked aligned because it sounds good. The build also refuses any claim of conformance to a standard the team hasn’t read: where a publisher’s site couldn’t be reached from the build environment, the claim is held back and the reason recorded.

The architecture itself is described using an enterprise architecture method, with the business, data, application and technology domains laid out on the same screen, twenty-one accepted architecture decision records with their rationale, and a risk register with 77 open and 44 resolved items. The same screen carries a register of the national framework that decides which suppliers an English practice can buy a foundation clinical system from, and it says on its face that every row was supplied and not read from the publisher, that commvita isn’t on that framework today, and that the module is rated behind, with the gap recorded as deployment, instead of left for a reader to notice. The tech stack, integrations and data models are tabs on the same page, so a reviewer doesn’t need a deck.

5 Every page has help written from its own registers, every build

The help guide has an entry for all 430 routed pages. None of it is typed. Each entry is generated from the platform’s own registers, and it is regenerated whenever a page changes.

Thirty-eight pages also carry a hand-written plain-English guide and are marked as such. The build fails if the register has drifted, or if any page changed after its guide was written. A guide that describes last month’s screen is worse than no guide, and the check is what stops it.

6 Where we stand against others

The competitive intelligence module rates each of 445 module entries against the products that do the same job, as ahead, on par or behind, and is updated every build. At build B-973, 175 are ahead of every product rated, 99 are on par somewhere and behind nowhere, and 171 are behind at least one.

Where a module is rated behind, the entry has to say what separates the two products and what would close the gap. The build enforces both. Claims the register used to make and withdrew after measurement stay on the record. Those 171 modules are behind across 405 individual comparisons. Most of them moved in October, when every rating of ahead or level on a screen that still shows demonstration data was checked against what a person can actually use on the platform. None of the 275 held, and 272 became behind. The full account of what each comparison is behind on, and why, is in the competitive intelligence explainer. It is our assessment and says so.

7 This website is held to the same rule

Every explainer on this site opens with the same line separating live from demonstrated, and every screenshot is captured from the running platform with the build stamped in the caption. The explainers say which they are showing, and when a screen is seeded they say so under the picture.

Where a page found a defect while it was being written, the defect is on the page. The research explainer names an extract that emits more people than its cohort. The architecture explainer prints the number of columns that still hold time as text. Our house rules for this site bar anything that can’t be true on the day it’s published, and a scoring tool checks each page for the tells of copy written to impress instead of inform.

What it costs, and the honest edges. Extreme transparency makes the platform look less finished than a competitor’s brochure, because the brochure isn’t measured. It means a deployability board that shows no jurisdiction deployable, a semantic registry with nothing signed off, and 98 seeded pages counted in public. Those are the edges of this page too. The registers describe the platform’s own estate, and the only competitive assessment on offer is ours.
