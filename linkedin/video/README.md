# Five LinkedIn videos — scripts, shot lists and the rules

Five films, two minutes each. The narration is written to be read out loud, so
the sentences are short and the words are ordinary ones. Where a technical word
is the real word, it gets explained once and then used.

**There are no video files.** These are scripts and shot lists. The screens have
to be recorded off the running platform, and the edit is yours.

## Before you record anything

**Screens come from the platform.** Record each shot through the product's own
screens and put the build stamp in the corner, the way the explainer
screenshots do. Never a mock-up. On video it's worse than on a page, because
nobody can pause a claim and check it.

**Say live or demonstration out loud.** Not in a caption at the bottom that
nobody reads on a phone. Where a shot is test data, the narration says so in the
same breath.

**Don't show how it's built.** Standing order, 20 September. No stack, no
tooling, no build steps. Results are fine — how long something took, how many
modules there are, what passes and what doesn't. Film 5 is the one that will
tempt you.

**Name no other supplier.** Not spoken, not on screen, not in a comparison.

**Captions.** Most of LinkedIn watches with the sound off. Burn the captions in,
and make the first three seconds work silently.

## 2 · Three ways of seeing the same system

**Status** LeftShift runs on test data and says so on every tab. The flow model
is live. Check each outcome tab before you film it.
**Routes** `/leftshift` · `/flow` · `/outcome-intelligence`

> Three screens. One record underneath. That's the whole argument.
>
> LeftShift measures the three big shifts a health system is asked to make.
> Paper to digital. Hospital to community. Treating illness to preventing it.
>
> A score for each one. Then one number overall.
>
> Now watch what it does next. It adjusts that number for who the gains are
> reaching.
>
> If the digital shift is being carried by people who were already easy to
> reach, the score comes down. The gap moves the headline number. It doesn't sit
> in an appendix at the back.
>
> This screen runs on test data today. There's a banner on every tab saying so.
> In a real deployment the numbers come from the live modules underneath. Which
> is the next screen.
>
> commvita Flow is the operational picture. How many people are waiting. How
> fast they're arriving. How long they stay. Station by station. And the one
> place holding everyone up, worked out from the model.
>
> Change the view to medicine, or surgery, or stroke, and the line redraws. The
> hold-up moves when you change what you're looking at.
>
> Outcome intelligence closes the loop. Did the change help? Who did it help?
> What did it cost?
>
> Here's why having all three on one platform matters.
>
> The shift you're measuring, the flow you're running and the outcome you're
> reporting are the same events, read three ways.
>
> Nobody reconciles anything. Nobody retypes anything. The board paper and the
> ward screen can't disagree, because there's only one of them.

## 3 · Standards: built to, and certified against

**Status** Built to, in every case. Not certified. The patient summary is live;
the cross-border connection is partial.
**Routes** `/standards` · `/ehds` · `/omop-cdm` · `/ig-spine`

> Let me split two words that get muddled. Built to a standard. Certified
> against one. We have the first and not the second.
>
> The record is built to openEHR's shapes. A finished consultation writes to a
> real openEHR store, and we ship one with the platform for each country.
>
> But it isn't a native openEHR system. We ship no archetypes. There's no query
> language. We say that on our own website.
>
> Why? Because anyone technical finds that gap in about ten minutes. And a claim
> that fails that test takes every other claim down with it.
>
> On the wire, FHIR. SNOMED for what was recorded. The medicines dictionary for
> drugs. OMOP for research, with the codes we couldn't map listed instead of
> quietly dropped.
>
> Europe. The law came in in March 2025. The technical detail is still being
> written, and the deadline for that is March 2027. The first data moves between
> countries in 2029.
>
> So nobody is certified against it. Nobody can be. It isn't finished.
>
> We're built to line up with it, and that's what we call it. The patient
> summary works today. The cross-border link waits on each country's own
> timetable, not ours.
>
> Same with the World Health Organization's architecture. We line up with it. We
> say we line up with it.
>
> And here's what we don't have. No security certification. No national toolkit
> return. No independent security test.
>
> Ask every supplier that question. Most won't answer it this directly.

## 4 · The cost aim, and what it's really about

**Status** The 80% figure is a published **aim**, not a result. No such
measurement has been run. The riskiest of the five — see the warning below.
**Routes** `/editions` · `/connector-dashboard` · `/golden-thread` · `/leftshift`

> I want to be careful here. There's a number attached, and the number is an
> aim.
>
> We want to take eighty per cent out of what this costs. That's the aim. Nobody
> has banked it. We haven't run that measurement and I won't pretend we have.
>
> Here's the thinking behind it.
>
> A health system doesn't buy one system. It buys a pile of them. A record here.
> A risk register there. Board papers. Incident reporting. Rostering. Analytics.
>
> Then you pay to connect them to each other.
>
> Then you pay for the kit underneath each one.
>
> Then you pay, every year, to prove each one is safe. Separately.
>
> The licences are the small half of that bill.
>
> One platform takes out the connecting work, because the incident on the ward
> and the risk on the board's register are the same thing. It takes out the
> duplicate kit. And it turns all that separate safety work into one job.
>
> The operational edition starts at a pound an instance. You host it. Every
> environment is included.
>
> But cost is the less interesting half.
>
> What I'd really argue for is better decisions.
>
> A decision made on half the picture is a guess wearing a confident face.
>
> When the record, the operations and the governance are the same data, the
> board paper matches the ward screen. Because there's only one of them.
>
> A risk score traces back to the exact events underneath it. An unfair gap
> moves the headline number instead of hiding behind it.
>
> Cheaper is a budget argument. Deciding on everything you know is a better one.

## 5 · From a letter to a tested capability

**Status** Martin's own account of his own build. **Check the two-week claim
against the commit record before filming.** It's the film's only hard number.
**Routes** `/waiting-well` · `/unified-waiting-list`
**Animation** `waiting-well-timeline.html` in this folder

> A letter went out about waiting lists. The point in it was simple. People
> waiting aren't a queue. They're a group of patients. And some of them are
> getting worse while they wait.
>
> Here's what normally happens next.
>
> Someone writes a requirement. It goes into a business case. The business case
> waits for a board meeting. Then procurement. Then a spec agreed with a
> supplier. Then it joins a release schedule. Then testing. Then it ships.
>
> Best case, months. Often longer than the policy that asked for it.
>
> Here's what happened instead. It was in a tested build in two weeks.
>
> This is what it does. While somebody waits: get them fitter for surgery, where
> that changes the result. Sort out the things that will complicate the
> operation — the blood sugar, the blood pressure, the anaemia nobody chased.
> Tell them where they are, which is most of what people want. And move them up
> if they get worse while they're waiting.
>
> Underneath it, one waiting list. So somebody waiting for three different
> things is one person, counted once.
>
> I'm not going to show you how we build. That's on purpose.
>
> What I will say is this. The code is written by AI and a person checks it. And
> the speed came from the model being right before the letter arrived.
>
> Waiting Well needed no new data model. The record already knew who was
> waiting, what for, and what else was wrong with them.
>
> That's the real claim. Not that we're fast. That getting the model right years
> earlier is what makes two weeks possible.

**Note on film 5.** The standing order bars publishing how the platform is
built. This script gives the elapsed time, which is a result, and stops. Don't
add tooling, step names or test figures in the edit. The animation leaves space
and the space will tempt somebody.
